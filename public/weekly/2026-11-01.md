# New Acquisitions: The Week of October 26 – November 1, 2026

*From the Library, with my compliments*

Dear Reader,

This week the Library travelled further than ever. We began in Russia, as we often do, with American eyewitnesses to the revolution and the treaty quarrels that strained the old friendship between Washington and St. Petersburg. Then we set out on a grand tour: Spain and the Spanish Americas, then Constantinople and the Bosphorus, then the German lands. We ended in China, Japan and Korea.

Along the way the shelves took in fifty-seven titles. Several are original-language editions, marked ★ below with their language: Spanish, French, German and Ottoman Turkish. A plain ★ Russia marks additions to our continuing Russia shelf. Some are among the most beautiful books ever printed. The Kelmscott Chaucer, Hooke's *Micrographia*, Haeckel's *Kunstformen der Natur* and Owen Jones's chromolithographed *Ancient Spanish Ballads* all arrived in a single week. I am also delighted to add Pierre Loti's *Aziyadé*, the novel of love and Ottoman Salonica and Stamboul that I have been reading myself with enormous pleasure. It pairs beautifully with Pardoe and Bartlett's *Beauties of the Bosphorus* and Melling's great *Voyage pittoresque*.

Happy reading,
*Eden*

## New on the Shelves

### Law (11)

- **Francis Lieber, *Legal and Political Hermeneutics*** (St. Louis, 1880): the first American treatise on statutory and constitutional interpretation, in Hammond's annotated edition.
- **★ Russia: *The Passport Question*** (American Jewish Committee, 1911): the dossier behind Taft's 1911 abrogation of the 1832 Russo-American commercial treaty.
- **Sir Henry Sumner Maine, *International Law*** (Holt, 1888): Maine's posthumous Cambridge lectures on sovereignty, naval belligerency and the laws of war.
- **Joseph Story, *Commentaries on the Conflict of Laws*** (Boston, 1834; first edition): the founding Anglo-American treatise on the subject, and the book that gave the field its name.
- **Francis Lieber, *Instructions for the Government of Armies of the United States in the Field*** (Van Nostrand, 1863): the Lieber Code, signed by Lincoln and the first modern codification of the laws of war.
- **★ Russia: Baron Roman Rosen, *Forty Years of Diplomacy*** (Knopf, 1922; 2 vols.): a Russian diplomat's memoir, from the 1863 Russian squadron at New York to his years in Washington.
- **Francisco de Vitoria, *De Indis et De Iure Belli Relectiones*** (Carnegie "Classics of International Law," 1917) [LA/EN]: the Salamanca lectures of 1539 that founded international law, in English with a facsimile of the 1696 Latin.
- **John H. Wigmore, *A Panorama of the World's Legal Systems*** (West, 1928; 3 vols.): a gorgeous illustrated tour of sixteen legal systems, from Egypt onward, with some 500 pictures.
- ***The Civil Law in Spain and Spanish-America*** (Washington, 1900): Spanish civil law as it stood in Cuba, Puerto Rico and the Philippines at the American handover.
- ***The Ottoman Land Code*** (tr. Ongley; London, 1892): the Tanzimat land law of 1858, which governed land tenure from Anatolia to Palestine, Iraq and Cyprus.
- **★[FR] George Young, *Corps de droit ottoman*, vol. I** (Oxford, 1905): the Capitulations and international acts that underpinned foreign privilege in the Empire.

### History (17)

- **★ Russia: Edmund Noble, *The Russian Revolt*** (Houghton Mifflin, 1885): a Boston journalist's anatomy of Nihilist Russia after his 1882 journey.
- **★ Russia: Arthur Bullard, *The Russian Pendulum*** (Macmillan, 1919): Petrograd and Kolchak's Siberia, seen by the head of the U.S. information service's Russian Division.
- **Abbot Gasquet, *The Greater Abbeys of England*** (1908): thirty-one abbeys, from Fountains and Rievaulx to Glastonbury and Whitby, with 60 colour plates by **Warwick Goble**.
- **★ Russia: John W. Bookwalter, *Siberia and Central Asia*** (1899): an Ohio manufacturer's 1898 journey on the new Trans-Siberian Railway and on to Samarkand.
- **★ Russia: *The Little Grandmother of the Russian Revolution*** (Little, Brown, 1917): Catherine Breshkovsky's reminiscences and letters, edited by Alice Stone Blackwell.
- **Jacob A. Riis, *How the Other Half Lives*** (Scribner, 1890): the founding work of photojournalism, illustrated from Riis's flash photographs of the tenements.
- **★ Russia: Emma Goldman, *My Disillusionment in Russia*** (Doubleday, Page, 1923): the deported anarchist's account of Soviet Russia in 1920–21, Kronstadt included.
- **Baron Davillier, *Spain*** (Bickers, 1881): **Gustave Doré**'s great travel book, with some 240 engravings of gypsies, bullfights, the Alhambra and Seville.
- **★[ES] Bernal Díaz del Castillo, *Historia verdadera de la conquista de la Nueva España*** (Madrid, 1632): the editio princeps of a foot-soldier's account of Cortés and Moctezuma, from the John Carter Brown copy.
- **Garcilaso de la Vega, el Inca, *Royal Commentaries of the Yncas*, vol. I** (Hakluyt Society, 1869): the Inca world from the inside, in its first English translation.
- **Howard Carter & A. C. Mace, *The Tomb of Tut·ankh·Amen*, vol. I** (Doran, 1923): Carter's own account of the discovery, with Harry Burton's photographs.
- **★[FR] Antoine-Ignace Melling, *Voyage pittoresque de Constantinople et des rives du Bosphore*: the plates** (Paris, Didot): perhaps the most beautiful view-book of Constantinople ever made, with its great engraved panoramas of palaces, mosques and the Seraglio.
- **Julia Pardoe, *The Beauties of the Bosphorus*** (Virtue, 1838): **W. H. Bartlett**'s steel engravings of mosques, kiosks and the Golden Horn.
- **Ogier Ghiselin de Busbecq, *Life and Letters*, vol. I** (1881): the "Turkish Letters" of the Habsburg envoy to Süleyman the Magnificent, the man who brought the tulip west.
- **George Catlin, *Letters and Notes on the North American Indians*, vol. I** (London, 1841): Catlin's years among the Plains tribes, with hundreds of engravings after his paintings.
- **★[FR] Théophile Gautier, *Constantinople*** (Michel Lévy, 1853; first edition): the great French aesthete's Stamboul, a perfect companion to Loti.
- **Evliya Efendi [Çelebi], *Narrative of Travels in Europe, Asia, and Africa*, vol. I** (Oriental Translation Fund, 1834): the seventeenth-century Ottoman traveller's account, in von Hammer's English.

### Literature (15)

- **Walt Whitman, *Leaves of Grass*** (McKay, 1891–92): the "deathbed" edition, the last text Whitman authorised.
- **★ Russia: Maxim Gorky, *Foma Gordyeeff*** (Scribner, 1901): Gorky's Volga-merchant novel in Isabel Hapgood's American translation.
- **★ Russia: Tolstoi, *The Kreutzer Sonata*** (Benj. R. Tucker, Boston, 1890): the translation the U.S. Post Office tried to ban, and which sold by the ten thousand.
- **Mark Twain, *Life on the Mississippi*** (Osgood, 1883): Twain's piloting apprenticeship and his return to the river, with more than 300 illustrations.
- **Stephen Crane, *The Red Badge of Courage*** (Appleton, 1896): an early printing in the first-issue Appleton format.
- **★[ES] Gustavo Adolfo Bécquer, *Obras*, t. I** (Madrid, 1871): his friends' posthumous memorial edition, which holds the *Leyendas*.
- **★[ES] Rubén Darío, *Azul…*** (Guatemala, 1890): the enlarged second edition of the book that launched *modernismo*.
- **★[ES] Rubén Darío, *Prosas profanas y otros poemas*** (Buenos Aires, 1896): the first edition.
- **J. G. Lockhart, *Ancient Spanish Ballads*** (Murray, 1841): **Owen Jones**'s chromolithographed borders and Moorish ornament make it one of the loveliest of Victorian books.
- **Lord Dunsany, *The Book of Wonder*** (Luce, 1915): jewelled fantasy tales with **S. H. Sime**'s eerie full-page drawings.
- **Lady Mary Wortley Montagu, *Letters*** (Becket & De Hondt, 1763; 3 vols.): the first edition of the Turkish Embassy Letters, which take in the hammam, the harem and smallpox inoculation.
- **E. J. W. Gibb, *Ottoman Poems*** (Trübner, 1882): Turkish verse rendered in its original forms, with the Turkish texts.
- **★[OT] Namık Kemal, *İntibāh*** ("Awakening"): one of the first Turkish novels, by the Young Ottoman poet of *Vatan*, printed in Ottoman Arabic script.
- **Geoffrey Chaucer, *Works*** (Kelmscott Press, 1896): William Morris's masterpiece, with 87 designs by **Burne-Jones**.
- **★[FR] Pierre Loti, *Aziyadé*** (Calmann Lévy, 1895 printing): the young naval officer's Stamboul romance, the book that made Loti.

### Science (14)

- **Vernon L. Kellogg, *Darwinism To-day*** (Holt, 1907): the state of the Darwinian debate at the century's turn.
- **Robert Kennedy Duncan, *The New Knowledge*** (1905): a popular account of the new physics and chemistry and the new theory of matter.
- **Sir Robert S. Ball, *Great Astronomers*** (Isbister, 1895): lives of astronomers from Ptolemy onward.
- **Asa Gray, *Darwiniana*** (Appleton, 1876): essays by Darwin's chief American ally.
- **T. C. Martin, *The Inventions, Researches and Writings of Nikola Tesla*** (1894): the first book on Tesla, compiled with his cooperation.
- **Heinrich Schliemann, *Ilios*** (Harper, 1881): the full report on Troy, with the famous autobiography of the grocer's boy who set out to find it.
- **Alexander von Humboldt, *Political Essay on the Kingdom of New Spain*, vol. I** (New York, 1811; first American edition): the founding work of Mexican geography.
- **John Lloyd Stephens, *Incidents of Travel in Central America, Chiapas, and Yucatan*, vol. I** (Harper, 1841): the rediscovery of Copán and Palenque, with **Frederick Catherwood**'s Maya engravings.
- **★[ES] Santiago Ramón y Cajal, *Recuerdos de mi vida*, t. II** (Madrid, 1917): the Nobel laureate's own account of discovering the neuron, illustrated with his drawings.
- **★[DE] Ernst Haeckel, *Kunstformen der Natur*** (Leipzig, 1899–1904): radiolaria, jellyfish and orchids as Art Nouveau ornament, across 100 plates.
- **Charles Fellows, *An Account of Discoveries in Lycia*** (Murray, 1841): the journey that found Xanthus and its tombs.
- **Robert Hooke, *Micrographia*** (London, 1665; first edition): the flea, the fly's eye and the first "cells", on great folding copperplates.
- **William Gilbert, *On the Loadstone and Magnetic Bodies*** (Wiley, 1893): *De Magnete*, the first great book of experimental physics, in its first full English translation.
- **Galileo, *The Sidereal Messenger*** (Rivingtons, 1880): the Moon's mountains and Jupiter's moons, in the first English translation of the *Sidereus Nuncius*.

## For the Collector: Buying Tips for Physical Copies

### 1. Pierre Loti, *Aziyadé*
The first edition (Calmann Lévy, 1879) appeared anonymously. Most copies you will meet are later Calmann Lévy printings with a "nième édition" statement on the title page. They make fine reading copies, especially in period half-leather. The real prizes are the illustrated editions of the 1920s and 1930s. French *livres d'artiste* are often issued in wrappers (*brochés*) and meant to be bound, so a copy "en feuilles" or in wrappers is normal, not a defect. Check that every plate called for is present.

### 3. J. G. Lockhart, *Ancient Spanish Ballads*
The book you want is the **1841 John Murray "new edition"** with Owen Jones's chromolithographed borders and title pages. It is often called the first chromolithographed book. The 1823 Blackwood first edition is unillustrated, and the many later Murray and American printings (1850s–1890s) vary widely in their decoration. Owen Jones copies are frequently found in elaborate Victorian presentation bindings. Check for loose plates and for colour offsetting onto facing pages.

### 4. Julia Pardoe & W. H. Bartlett, *The Beauties of the Bosphorus*
It was issued in parts from 1838 by George Virtue, then bound in one or two quarto volumes, and reprinted for decades. A complete copy has an engraved title, a map of the Bosphorus, portraits and 80-odd steel engravings. **Collate before you buy.** This is a classic "breaker", and many copies have lost plates to the print trade. One copy listed this week is clearly described as *without the illustrations*, which leaves the text alone. Foxing on steel engravings is almost universal, so price accordingly. Beware too of modern print-on-demand "leather-bound" facsimiles listed under the original dates.

### 5. Gasquet & Goble, *The Greater Abbeys of England*
This is a Warwick Goble gift book, published by Chatto & Windus in London and Dodd, Mead in New York in 1908. Check that all 60 colour plates are present and firmly attached. The trade edition is easy to find and very affordable. The great rarity is the **special edition in full vellum, limited to 60 copies**. Vellum tends to bow and discolour, and surviving dust jackets from 1908 are exceptional.

Incomplete sets and single plates are common, and so are modern reprints listed as "1904". For most readers the Dover selection is the sensible way to have these images on paper.

## Coming Into the Public Domain on January 1, 2027

Works first published in the United States in 1931 enter the U.S. public domain on New Year's Day. Two caveats apply. The 95-year term applies only where the copyright was properly **renewed**, so many unrenewed 1931 works are already free. And **sound recordings and films follow different rules**: the recordings that come free this January are those of 1926. Foreign works are generally treated the same way in the U.S., but status abroad differs. Here are some of the titles I look forward to adding:

- **Pearl S. Buck, *The Good Earth***: the Pulitzer novel of Chinese peasant life, a natural successor to this week's China shelf.
- **William Faulkner, *Sanctuary***: Faulkner's darkest and most notorious novel.
- **Virginia Woolf, *The Waves***: her most experimental novel, six voices from childhood to age.
- **Dashiell Hammett, *The Glass Key***: Hammett's novel of big-city politics and loyalty.
- **★[FR] Antoine de Saint-Exupéry, *Vol de nuit***: the night airmail over Patagonia, with Gide's preface. It won the Prix Femina in 1931.
- **★[FR] Georges Simenon, *Pietr-le-Letton***: the first Maigret.
- **★[FR] Jean de Brunhoff, *Histoire de Babar***: the little elephant's first picture book.
- **★[JA] Suihō Tagawa, *Norakuro***: the comic-strip adventures of a stray-dog soldier, a landmark of early manga.
- **Erich Maria Remarque, *The Road Back***: the sequel to *All Quiet on the Western Front*.
- **Benjamin N. Cardozo, *Law and Literature and Other Essays and Addresses*** (Harcourt, Brace): the great judge as essayist.
- **Karl N. Llewellyn, "Some Realism About Realism"** (44 *Harvard Law Review* 1222): the manifesto of American legal realism.
- **Kurt Gödel, "Über formal unentscheidbare Sätze…"** (*Monatshefte für Mathematik und Physik*): the incompleteness theorems.
- **Frederick Lewis Allen, *Only Yesterday***: the classic informal history of the 1920s.
- **★ Russia: Maurice Hindus, *Red Bread***: a Russian-born American writer returns to the countryside in the years of collectivisation.

*And in passing:* the songs "As Time Goes By," "Minnie the Moocher" and "Brother, Can You Spare a Dime?" enter the public domain as compositions. Salvador Dalí's *The Persistence of Memory* (1931) follows suit for U.S. purposes. Among the 1931 films are *Frankenstein*, *Dracula*, Chaplin's *City Lights*, Fritz Lang's *M*, *The Public Enemy*, the Marx Brothers' *Monkey Business* and Murnau's *Tabu*. Films are a special case, though: the 1931 film itself becomes free, but the novels, characters and music it draws on may not.
